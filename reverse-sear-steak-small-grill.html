Ribeye on a Small Charcoal Grill: Sear and Slide

A thick, well-marbled ribeye is the best argument for cooking over charcoal, and a small grill is a great tool for it: the food sits close to the coals, and the 12-gauge carbon steel of the Slot Grill Compact holds heat for a hard sear. The catch is thickness. A 1¼" steak left over hot coals the whole time chars outside before the middle comes up. The answer is sear and slide: crust it over the hot zone, then slide it to the cool zone to finish gently. You get a deep crust and an even, rosy middle without a lid, a dial or guesswork.

Serves: 2 · Total time: About 30 minutes active, plus optional 40+ minutes salting · Grill: Slot Grill Compact

Why this works on the Compact

  • Close, intense heat for a fast crust.
  • Thick steel that holds heat when cold meat hits the grate.
  • A two-piece top, so you can run a grate plus a griddle for a butter baste or onions.
  • A cool zone that works like the oven half of a restaurant sear-and-finish.

Ingredients

  • 2 ribeye steaks, about 1¼" thick (bone-in or boneless)
  • Coarse salt, about ¾ teaspoon per steak
  • Freshly ground black pepper
  • High-smoke-point oil
  • Optional: 2 tablespoons butter, 2 cloves garlic, a sprig of rosemary or thyme
  • Optional for serving: flaky salt

Gear

  • Slot Grill Compact, seasoned (how)
  • Chimney starter, charcoal or cooking wood, newspaper or natural fire starters, a lighter
  • Long tongs and a sturdy spatula
  • Heat-safe gloves
  • Instant-read thermometer
  • Metal can with a lid for cold ash
  • Sheet pan or foil pan for raw-to-grill and a clean one for cooked food

Fire setup on the Compact

The Compact's cooking area is 16.25" × 9" (146 sq in). That is plenty for one to three people, but it means coal placement does most of the work a dial does on other cookers. Use a two-zone fire: lit coals banked across about two-thirds of the length, the last third thin or empty. The hot side sears; the cool side finishes, holds and rescues food from flare-ups. Light with a chimney or one of the no-lighter-fluid methods.

Step by step

  1. Dry brine. At least 40 minutes before cooking (or up to overnight, uncovered on a rack in the fridge), salt both sides and edges. If you're short on time, salt right before cooking instead. Salting 10–20 minutes ahead is the least effective window.
  2. Light a full chimney. Steak wants a hot zone. Lump charcoal is great here.
  3. Build two zones. When the coals are ashed over, pile them across two-thirds of the Compact's firebox, deeper at the far end. Leave the last third empty.
  4. Preheat and oil the grates over the coals for several minutes.
  5. Pat the steaks dry, rub with a thin film of oil, and add pepper.
  6. Sear. Lay the steaks over the deepest coals. Leave them until they release, about 2 minutes, then flip. Sear the second side. Hold the fat cap edge against the grate with tongs for a minute to render it.
  7. Slide. Move the steaks to the cool end. Flip every minute or two and check the thickest part with an instant-read thermometer.
  8. Pull early. Take the steaks off a few degrees below your target; the temperature keeps rising as they rest.
  9. Optional butter baste. Set a small pan or the griddle at the cool end, melt butter with garlic and herbs, and spoon it over the steaks for the last minute.
  10. Rest 5 minutes on a clean board, then slice across the grain and sprinkle with flaky salt.

Doneness guide (pull temperatures)

Many cooks pull steak at these temperatures and let it rise during the rest. USDA's minimum for beef steaks is 145°F with a 3-minute rest; choose what you're comfortable with.

Variations

  • Strip or sirloin: same method; leaner steaks flare less and cook a little faster.
  • Thin steaks (under 1"): skip the slide. Sear hot on both sides and pull.
  • Reverse order (true reverse sear): start on the cool side until about 15°F below target, then sear hard over the coals. Great for very thick steaks.
  • Steak and eggs: run a griddle on the cool side and fry eggs while the steaks rest. See camp breakfast.
  • Wood flavor: add a small chunk of untreated hardwood to the hot coals right before searing.

Troubleshooting

ProblemFix
Gray band under the crustToo long over hot coals. Sear shorter, finish on the cool side.
Crust won't formSteak wet or coals not ready. Pat dry; wait for ashed coals; preheat grates.
Big flare-ups from the fatMove to the cool side until they stop; render the fat cap edge-on briefly.
Overshot the temperaturePull earlier next time; carryover during the rest adds several degrees.

Serving, leftovers and cleanup

Slice across the grain. Leftover steak makes the best fajitas or steak-and-egg tacos the next morning. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Once the grill is cold, scrape the grates and wipe them with a light coat of oil.

Safe internal temperatures

Guessing is how small-grill cooks go wrong, because everything happens fast over a close fire. Use an instant-read thermometer in the thickest part, away from bone. USDA minimum internal temperatures:

FoodMinimum internal temp
Beef, pork, veal and lamb steaks, chops and roasts145°F, then rest 3 minutes
Ground beef, pork, veal and lamb160°F
Poultry (whole, parts, ground)165°F
Fish and shellfish145°F, or flesh opaque and separates easily with a fork
Egg dishes160°F; cook eggs until yolk and white are firm
Leftovers and casseroles165°F

Taking this recipe on the road

The Compact packs about 1" thick and weighs about 14 lb, so this recipe travels to a campsite, tailgate, river bank or job site as easily as it cooks on the patio. A few habits make it safer and easier away from a kitchen:

  • Prep at home. Trim, season or marinate meat, chop vegetables and mix sauces in the kitchen. Pack each in a sealed bag or container labeled by cook order.
  • Keep raw meat cold and separate. Pack it at the bottom of the cooler, sealed, below ready-to-eat food. Keep the cooler at 40°F or below with plenty of ice, and keep it in the shade.
  • Two sets of tongs and platters: one for raw, one for cooked. Never put cooked food back on the tray that held raw meat.
  • Bring water and soap for hands and tools, plus paper towels and a trash bag.
  • Pack the thermometer. It matters more outdoors, where wind and cold change cook times.
  • Plan for ash. Bring a metal can with a lid and follow the venue's disposal rules.

Quick-start checklist

  • Grill seasoned and dry; old ash emptied
  • Stable, level, non-combustible spot with clearance
  • Charcoal lit in a chimney, ashed over
  • Two-zone fire built, grates preheated and oiled
  • Thermometer, tongs, spatula and gloves within reach
  • Clean tray ready for cooked food

Common small-grill mistakes (and the fix)

  1. Cooking before the coals are ready. Black-topped coals give smoky, weak heat and sticking. Wait until they glow under a light coat of gray ash.
  2. Wall-to-wall coals. With no cool zone, every flare-up becomes a burn. Leave at least a third of the firebox thin or empty.
  3. Crowding the grate. 146 sq in fills fast. Leave space between pieces so you can move them, and cook in waves.
  4. Moving food too early. Let it sear and release on its own; it sticks until a crust forms.
  5. Cold, dirty grates. Preheat for a few minutes, scrape, and wipe with an oiled paper towel held in tongs.
  6. Wet food. Pat meat and vegetables dry before oiling and seasoning. Moisture steams instead of searing.
  7. Judging doneness by time or color. Small fires vary. Use a thermometer.
  8. Leaving ash in the firebox. Old ash chokes airflow. Empty it once cold before the next cook.
  9. Putting the grill away wet. Raw carbon steel needs to be dried and lightly oiled. See rust prevention.

Frequently asked questions

How long do I cook a ribeye on a small charcoal grill?

It depends on thickness and fire, so cook to temperature. A 1¼ inch ribeye typically gets about 2 minutes per side over hot coals, then several more minutes on the cool side until it reaches your target internal temperature.

Do I need a lid to cook a thick steak?

No. A two-zone fire lets you sear over direct heat and finish over indirect heat on an open grill like the Slot Grill Compact.

When should I salt steak?

At least 40 minutes before cooking, or right before cooking if you are short on time.

What temperature should I pull a medium-rare steak?

Many cooks pull at about 125 to 130°F and rest it to about 130 to 135°F. USDA's minimum for beef steaks is 145°F with a 3-minute rest.
