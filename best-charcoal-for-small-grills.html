Charcoal vs. Wood on a Small Grill: What to Burn

The Slot Grill Compact burns charcoal or cooking wood. Both work, but they behave differently in a small open firebox, and picking the right one for the cook makes a bigger difference on a 146 sq in grill than on a big one. Here's what to burn, how much, and how to handle it.

Briquettes

Briquettes are compressed charcoal with binders, shaped for uniform size. In a small firebox that uniformity is a real advantage: they stack evenly, burn at a predictable rate, and make temperature control easier. They do make more ash, which matters on a compact grill because ash sitting under the coals chokes airflow. Empty it between cooks.

Let briquettes fully ash over in the chimney before pouring. Half-lit briquettes smell and taste off and give weak heat.

Lump charcoal

Lump is wood that's been charred into charcoal without binders. It lights quickly, burns hotter, and leaves less ash, which is why many people like it for steak. The tradeoffs: it burns faster, pieces are uneven (break big chunks so they sit flat), and the bag usually has dust at the bottom. Leave the dust in the bag; it smothers airflow.

Cooking wood

Untreated, seasoned hardwood splits work in the Compact too. Build a small fire, let it burn down to glowing coals, then cook over the coals rather than the flames. Wood gives the most flavor and the most campfire feel, but it takes longer to be ready and needs feeding. Keep splits small enough to fit inside the firebox without hanging over the sides.

Many cooks mix fuels: a bed of charcoal for steady heat with a small split or a few wood chunks added for flavor.

How much fuel?

CookStarting fuel (guide)
Quick weeknight for 1–2 (burgers, a steak, sausages)About a half chimney
Bone-in chicken, chops, wings, or two waves of foodA fuller chimney, plus a few coals added to the hot side later
Cooking for a crowdA full chimney to start, with a second chimney lighting for top-ups
Griddle-only breakfastA modest, even bed under the griddle

These are starting points. Wind, cold weather and damp fuel all mean you need more. It's always easier to add lit coals than to take heat away on a small grill.

Storing fuel

  • Keep charcoal dry. Damp charcoal is miserable to light and burns weakly. A lidded bin or a dry bag works for trips.
  • Roll the bag closed after use.
  • Store away from the grill area and any heat source.

Never burn

Treated, painted or stained lumber, pallets of unknown origin, plywood, trash, plastic, or anything you can't identify. They can release toxic fumes onto your food. Use only real charcoal or untreated cooking wood, and skip lighter fluid (it flares in wind and can taint food). See how to light without it.

Quick-start checklist

  • Grill seasoned and dry; old ash emptied
  • Stable, level, non-combustible spot with clearance
  • Charcoal lit in a chimney, ashed over
  • Two-zone fire built, grates preheated and oiled
  • Thermometer, tongs, spatula and gloves within reach
  • Clean tray ready for cooked food

Reading your coals

  • Black with flames: still lighting. Wait.
  • Red glow, light gray ash: peak heat. Sear now.
  • Thick white ash, dim glow: fading. Good for gentle cooking, desserts, or time to add fuel.
  • Small and crumbling: nearly spent. Consolidate or let the fire burn out.

Smoke and flavor

Clean-burning, fully lit charcoal gives a mild, pleasant smoke. Thick white or acrid smoke usually means unlit fuel, fat dripping on coals, or damp wood. A small chunk of untreated hardwood on hot coals adds aroma for short cooks.

Safety basics (every cook)

  • Outdoors only. Never use a charcoal grill indoors, in a garage, tent, camper, boat cabin or vehicle. Burning charcoal makes carbon monoxide, which you cannot see or smell.
  • Stable, level, non-combustible surface. Bare soil, gravel, sand, concrete, pavers or a fire-safe pad. The Compact's 2⅞" air gap between the table and the bottom of the coal bed helps minimize radiant heat to the surface below, but it is still a live fire.
  • Clearance. Keep it away from tents, vehicles, siding, overhangs, dry grass and anything that can burn. Keep kids and pets back.
  • Follow local rules. Burn bans, campground, park, venue and HOA rules come first.
  • Water or a fire extinguisher nearby, plus heat-safe gloves for handling grates and panels.
  • Ash stays hot for a long time. Put it in a metal container with a lid and never pack hot steel or ash into a vehicle.

Common small-grill mistakes (and the fix)

  1. Cooking before the coals are ready. Black-topped coals give smoky, weak heat and sticking. Wait until they glow under a light coat of gray ash.
  2. Wall-to-wall coals. With no cool zone, every flare-up becomes a burn. Leave at least a third of the firebox thin or empty.
  3. Crowding the grate. 146 sq in fills fast. Leave space between pieces so you can move them, and cook in waves.
  4. Moving food too early. Let it sear and release on its own; it sticks until a crust forms.
  5. Cold, dirty grates. Preheat for a few minutes, scrape, and wipe with an oiled paper towel held in tongs.
  6. Wet food. Pat meat and vegetables dry before oiling and seasoning. Moisture steams instead of searing.
  7. Judging doneness by time or color. Small fires vary. Use a thermometer.
  8. Leaving ash in the firebox. Old ash chokes airflow. Empty it once cold before the next cook.
  9. Putting the grill away wet. Raw carbon steel needs to be dried and lightly oiled. See rust prevention.

After the cook: burn-down and pack-down

  1. Let the remaining coals burn down. Don't leave a fire unattended.
  2. When ash and steel are completely cold, dump the ash into a metal container with a lid, or dispose of it where the venue or campground allows.
  3. Scrape the grates, then wipe the cooking surfaces with a light coat of oil or Slot Grill Seasoning Balm.
  4. Lift off the grates, slide out the V side panels, pull the end pieces and stack. The Compact packs to about 1" thick.
  5. Store it dry. A bag or old box keeps soot off the rest of your gear.

Frequently asked questions

Can the Slot Grill Compact burn wood?

Yes. Slot Grill lists charcoal or cooking wood as fuel for the Compact. Use untreated, seasoned hardwood and cook over the coals once the flames die down.

Is lump charcoal or briquettes better for a small grill?

Briquettes give steadier, longer, more predictable heat; lump lights faster, burns hotter and leaves less ash. Many cooks use briquettes for chicken and long cooks, and lump for steak sears.

How much charcoal do I need?

About a half chimney is a good start for a quick cook for one or two on the Compact. Use more for bone-in meats, groups, wind or cold weather.

Can I mix wood and charcoal?

Yes. A charcoal bed with a few hardwood chunks or a small split gives steady heat plus wood flavor.
